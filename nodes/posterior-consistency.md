# Posterior Consistency

## Summary

Posterior consistency is the property that, as the amount of data grows without bound, the [[bayes-rule]] posterior collapses onto the *true* value of the unknown quantity — no matter where the prior started, as long as the prior was not dogmatic (did not rule that value out in advance). The reason lives inside `posterior ∝ likelihood × prior`: the likelihood carries the data, and its contribution keeps growing as observations accumulate, while the prior contributes a single fixed amount set once at the start. So the data eventually dominates and drags the posterior to the truth, leaving the prior's influence to fade. A sharper statement, the **Bernstein–von Mises** result, says the limiting shape is approximately a bell curve centred on the true value, with its width shrinking like `1/√n` in the sample size `n` — the posterior does not merely point at the truth, it tightens around it. The practical lesson is reassuring: a merely *wrong* prior only slows early learning; it cannot mislead you in the limit, so you usually do not hand-edit a prior — you let data overwhelm it.

## Grounded explanation

### What the property is

Recall the inference setup from [[bayes-rule]]. There is an unknown quantity `θ` we want to learn (a "parameter" — a number, fixed but unknown, that indexes the state of the world; for a coin, `θ` is `p`, the unknown probability of heads). We hold a **prior** distribution `P(θ)` over its possible values before seeing data, observe data `D`, and form the **posterior** `P(θ | D) ∝ P(D | θ) · P(θ)` — the prior reweighted by the [[likelihood]] `P(D | θ)`, how well each candidate value of `θ` predicted what we saw.

Now imagine the data is not a fixed batch but a stream that keeps arriving, and write `n` for the number of observations seen so far. Each value of `n` gives a different posterior. Suppose, hypothetically, that the data is genuinely generated by one *true* value `θ★`. **Posterior consistency** is the statement that, as `n → ∞`, the sequence of posteriors *concentrates* at `θ★`: the probability mass piles up arbitrarily close to `θ★`, and the mass anywhere else shrinks to zero. "Concentrates" is the key word — the posterior is a distribution, and we are claiming that distribution becomes a tall narrow spike sitting on the truth. Crucially, this holds *regardless of which prior we started from* (subject to one condition stated below). Two analysts who began with very different priors end up at the same place once the data is plentiful.

### Why it happens: the likelihood grows, the prior does not

The mechanism is already visible in `posterior ∝ likelihood × prior`. The two factors scale with the data very differently.

For independent observations the joint likelihood is a *product* of one factor per observation, `P(D | θ) = P(d₁ | θ) · P(d₂ | θ) · … · P(dₙ | θ)`. Every new observation multiplies in another factor, so the likelihood's pull on `θ` accumulates and strengthens as `n` climbs — it is built from `n` pieces and grows with `n`. The prior `P(θ)`, by contrast, is a single fixed factor chosen once before any data; it never grows. It enters the product exactly once and stays put.

So in the product, an ever-larger stack of likelihood factors multiplies against one unchanging prior factor. As `n` increases the likelihood term comes to dominate utterly: whichever value of `θ` best explains the growing pile of data is multiplied up most sharply, and because the data is truly generated by `θ★`, that best-explaining value is `θ★` itself. The prior can shift *where the posterior sits* while data is scarce — it can tilt early conclusions toward its own bias — but it contributes a bounded, fixed amount, and a bounded amount cannot hold out against a factor that keeps compounding. This is the precise sense in which a prior "washes out": its single fixed contribution becomes negligible beside the accumulating likelihood, so the posterior is dragged onto the truth and the starting point is forgotten.

This also says exactly *why a "wrong" prior is harmless in the limit but not instantly*. A prior that initially favours the wrong region biases the posterior while `n` is small — it slows learning, because the early likelihood factors must first overcome the prior's head start. But every additional observation tips the product further toward `θ★`, so the bias is transient. The prior costs you speed, never the destination.

### The one condition: the prior must not be dogmatic

There is a single requirement, and it follows directly from the proportionality. Wherever the prior is *exactly* zero, the posterior is forced to zero there too, because `posterior ∝ likelihood × prior` and anything times zero is zero — for every `n`, no matter how large. No quantity of data can revive a value the prior assigned probability zero, since the data only ever multiplies the prior, and it cannot multiply nothing into something. A **dogmatic** prior is one that places zero probability on some value; if it does this to the *true* value `θ★`, consistency fails permanently — the posterior can never concentrate on a point it was told in advance is impossible. The cure is simply to keep the prior strictly positive everywhere the truth might lie. (That failure case, and the rule against it, is treated separately; here we only need that a *non-dogmatic* prior — positive on all candidate values — is what consistency requires.)

### Bernstein–von Mises: the limiting shape and rate

Consistency says *where* the posterior ends up (on `θ★`); the **Bernstein–von Mises** statement says *what it looks like* on the way there. For large `n`, the posterior is approximately a **normal distribution** — the symmetric bell curve — centred on the true value `θ★`, and its spread narrows in proportion to `1/√n`. The spread of a distribution measures how far its mass typically strays from the centre; saying it shrinks like `1/√n` means that to halve the posterior's width you need roughly four times the data, since `1/√(4n) = (1/2)·(1/√n)`. So the posterior does two things at once as data accumulates: it *centres* on the truth (consistency) and it *tightens* around it (the `1/√n` rate). A tightening bell on the true value is the concrete picture of "concentrates at the truth," and it is why we grow increasingly *certain* — not just increasingly *correct* — as `n` rises.

### Worked instance: a biased prior washed out by 1000 coin flips

Take a coin whose true probability of heads is `θ★ = p = 0.7`, and suppose we start with a prior that is badly wrong about it.

For a coin the natural prior is a **Beta distribution**, written `Beta(α, β)`. Its two parameters `α` and `β` act as *pseudo-counts*: `α` behaves like a number of heads "imagined seen" before any real data and `β` like imagined tails, and its mean (the value it expects for `p`) is `α / (α + β)`. The reason this prior is convenient is the update rule that falls straight out of `posterior ∝ likelihood × prior`: after observing `h` real heads and `t` real tails, the posterior is again a Beta, with the counts simply added in — `Beta(α + h, β + t)`. (The real data counts add to the imagined pseudo-counts; that is all the update does.)

Choose a deliberately misleading prior: `Beta(2, 8)`. Its mean is `2 / (2 + 8) = 0.2` — it expects a coin heavily weighted toward *tails*, the opposite of the truth `0.7`. This is a "wrong" prior, but not dogmatic: `Beta(2, 8)` is positive on every value in `(0, 1)`, so it never rules `0.7` out, and consistency applies.

Now flip the coin `n = 1000` times. Since the true `p = 0.7`, we expect about `h ≈ 700` heads and `t ≈ 300` tails. Apply the update by adding counts:

> posterior `= Beta(α + h, β + t) = Beta(2 + 700, 8 + 300) = Beta(702, 308)`.

Its mean is `702 / (702 + 308) = 702 / 1010 ≈ 0.695`. Compare the three numbers:

- the prior mean was `0.2` (the wrong belief we started with),
- the truth is `0.7`,
- the posterior mean is `≈ 0.695` — essentially on the truth.

The pseudo-counts `2` and `8` are now drowned by the real counts `700` and `300`; they shift the answer from `700/1000 = 0.700` only to `0.695`, a difference of `0.005`. That residue is the entire remaining trace of a prior that began a full `0.5` away from the truth — the prior has been washed out, exactly as the growing-likelihood argument predicts. And the posterior `Beta(702, 308)` is not just centred near `0.7`; it is sharply peaked there (large pseudo-totals make a narrow Beta), illustrating the Bernstein–von Mises tightening: with `1000` observations the bell is already narrow, and another `4000` flips would halve its width again. So we need not "fix" the bad prior by hand — the data corrected it for us.
