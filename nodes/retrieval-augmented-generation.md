# Retrieval-Augmented Generation

## Summary

**Retrieval-augmented generation** (RAG) is a way of making a language model answer a question using *external* knowledge — text it was never trained on — by **retrieving** the few most relevant passages and placing them into the model's [[context-window]] before it generates its reply. The model reads those passages as part of its input and grounds its answer in them, rather than relying only on the facts baked into its weights during training. The retrieval engine is a [[vector-database]]: offline, a body of documents is split into chunks and stored as vectors; online, the user's question is turned into a vector, the database returns the most similar chunks, and those chunks are pasted into the prompt. RAG exists to overcome two hard limits at once — the [[context-window]] is finite, so a whole corpus cannot simply be fed in; and the model's built-in knowledge is frozen at training time, so it cannot know anything private or recent. RAG supplies exactly the relevant slice of fresh, private, or oversized knowledge on demand, with no retraining.

## Grounded explanation

Begin from the two walls a bare language model runs into, because RAG is defined by the fact that it climbs both of them.

The first wall comes from the [[context-window]]: the model can only attend over at most some fixed number of tokens — the small chunks of text that are its atomic units of input — at once, a hard ceiling quoted in thousands of tokens, and anything outside the window does not exist for the model unless it is supplied again as fresh input. This means you *cannot* solve the knowledge problem by simply pasting an entire corpus into the prompt. A library of ten thousand documents is millions of tokens; it will not fit, not in an 8K window and not in a 1M one, and even where it nominally fits the cost is prohibitive, because attention cost grows with the square of the token count. So whatever knowledge we hand the model, we must hand it a *small* amount — only the part that matters for the question in front of us.

The second wall is what the model knows on its own. A language model's facts are **parametric knowledge**: they are absorbed into the model's numeric weights during training and then frozen there. That has two consequences. First, the knowledge is stale — it reflects the world as of the training cutoff and cannot include anything that happened afterward. Second, it is necessarily public and general — the model was never trained on your company's internal wiki, your private documents, or this morning's news, so those facts are simply not in it. When asked about such things a bare model does not refuse; it does worse, producing fluent but unfounded text — a **hallucination** — because it has no source to draw on and no way to say "I was not trained on this."

RAG is the architecture that answers both walls with one move: *fetch the small, relevant slice of an external knowledge store and put it inside the window, so the model reads it as input instead of recalling it from weights.* The central object of the concept is therefore not the model and not the database in isolation, but the **pipeline** that joins them — a retrieval step feeding a generation step. It splits into two phases, one done once ahead of time and one done per question.

The **offline (indexing) phase** prepares the knowledge so it can be searched by meaning. The corpus — whatever body of text we want the model to be able to use — is first split into **chunks**: passages of a manageable size, roughly paragraph-length, each small enough that several of them together will fit inside the [[context-window]] alongside the question. Each chunk is then turned into an [[embedding]] — a dense vector that encodes its meaning — and all those vectors are loaded into a [[vector-database]] — the store built to hold large collections of such meaning-vectors and to answer "which stored vectors are most similar to this query vector?" quickly, with the original chunk text kept attached as metadata. This phase is paid once; from then on the knowledge is sitting in a form that can be queried by similarity in milliseconds.

The **online (query-time) phase** runs every time a user asks something. The question is turned into an [[embedding]] by the *same* procedure used on the chunks, so that it lives in the same space and geometric closeness between the question's vector and a chunk's vector means the chunk is about what the question asks. That query vector is sent to the [[vector-database]], which returns the **top-k** most similar chunks — the `k` nearest neighbors, where `k` is a small number like five. Those few chunk texts are then **concatenated** into the prompt: the system assembles one input consisting of the retrieved chunks plus the user's question (typically with a short instruction such as "answer using the passages below"), and crucially this whole assembled prompt must fit inside the [[context-window]] — which is exactly why we retrieve only a handful of chunks rather than the whole corpus. Finally the model **generates** its answer reading those chunks as context, so its reply is *grounded* in supplied text rather than spun from frozen weights.

Now the why, stated as the invariant the design maintains: *the model only ever needs to hold the few passages relevant to the current question, never the whole corpus.* This is the single insight that makes RAG work, and it is what lets a finite window reach an unbounded body of knowledge. The corpus can be arbitrarily large and can change every day; none of that touches the model, because the model never sees the corpus — it sees only the top-k slice the retriever selected for this one question, and that slice is sized to fit. The "magic-looking" step that deserves justification is *why retrieving only k chunks does not throw away the answer.* The justification is that relevance is concentrated: for a well-posed question, the passages that actually bear on it are few, and a [[vector-database]] is precisely the instrument that finds those few by meaning-similarity rather than by exact keyword match, so the small retrieved set reliably contains the evidence the question needs. We are not hoping a random handful of chunks happens to help; we are asking a similarity index for the handful most likely to.

From this fall the properties people value in RAG, each a direct consequence of the mechanism rather than an add-on. Knowledge can be **fresh and private**: to teach the system something new you add its chunks to the [[vector-database]], and the next query can retrieve them — no retraining, because the new facts enter through the window, not through the weights. Answers can **cite their evidence**: since every fact the model used arrived as a named retrieved chunk, the system can show which source chunk an answer came from, and this is what curbs hallucination — the model is steered to answer from passages in front of it and the user can check them, instead of trusting unfounded recall. And it **scales past the window**: a corpus far too large to fit is reachable because only its relevant slice is ever loaded.

Make it concrete with a non-degenerate instance: a question asked over a corpus of **10,000 documents**. Offline, suppose those documents split into, say, 200,000 paragraph-sized chunks; each is embedded and the 200,000 vectors are loaded into a [[vector-database]] with the chunk text attached — a one-time cost. The corpus is millions of tokens, so it could never be pasted into any [[context-window]] whole. Now compare the two systems on the question. The **bare model** has no access to these 10,000 documents — they are not in its parametric knowledge (they may be private, or written after its training cutoff) — so it either declines or, more often, hallucinates a plausible-sounding but unsourced answer. The **RAG system** instead converts the question into a query [[embedding]], asks the [[vector-database]] for the top `k = 5` nearest chunks (it traverses its similarity index and returns the 5 chunks closest in meaning, out of all 200,000, in milliseconds), and concatenates those 5 chunk texts with the question into one prompt. If a chunk averages 200 tokens, the retrieved evidence is about 5 × 200 = 1,000 tokens, which together with the question sits comfortably inside even an 8K [[context-window]] — whereas the full corpus would not fit in a window a hundred times larger. The model then generates its answer from those 5 chunks and can quote the exact passage it relied on. Same model, same window; the only difference is that RAG put the right 1,000 tokens in front of it instead of leaving it to guess from frozen weights. That difference — relevant external evidence delivered into a finite window on demand — is the whole of what retrieval-augmented generation contributes.

Two placements, kept as plain prose because they are neighbors of the concept rather than parts of it. *Fine-tuning* is the alternative people often weigh against RAG: it bakes new knowledge into the weights by further training, changing the model's parametric knowledge, whereas RAG leaves the weights untouched and injects knowledge through the window at query time — so RAG updates instantly by editing the store, while fine-tuning requires a training run. And *agent memory* — an assistant that stores embeddings of what it has seen and later recalls the relevant ones — is RAG's same retrieve-then-use loop pointed at the assistant's own past rather than at a document corpus. Both are uses or variants surrounding the pipeline this node describes; the pipeline itself is just retrieve relevant chunks, place them in the window, generate.
